# Production and review

A strong artifact requires a workflow that separates analytical truth, executive translation, design, approval, and release. AI can accelerate each stage, but it cannot own the judgment.

## Use a gated production workflow

1. **Decision brief** — audience, authority, meeting outcome, timing, sensitivity.
2. **Evidence inventory** — claims, states, sources, cutoff dates, assumptions, unresolved conflicts.
3. **Story spine** — decision, motivation, concrete case, mechanism, evidence, options, uncertainty, execution.
4. **Low-fidelity pages** — titles, page jobs, rough visuals, appendix routing.
5. **Content review** — technical owner, business owner, risk/legal where applicable.
6. **Design build** — grid, typography, charts, tables, accessibility, navigation.
7. **Red-team review** — skeptical reader, numeric audit, claim-to-source audit, adverse interpretation.
8. **Release gate** — correct identity, status, permissions, links, format, distribution list.
9. **Meeting and record** — decision captured, conditions, owner, date, follow-up measure.

Don't design the final pages before the story spine is stable, but do sketch visuals early — a visual often reveals that the analysis lacks a comparator, denominator, or mechanism. Assign one publication owner: multiple contributors can own analyses and sections, but one person must own the coherence and release state of the artifact.

## A 48-hour rescue workflow for a difficult meeting

When a dense technical document must be made executive-ready quickly, protect the highest-leverage moves.

**First four hours** — write the exact decision and meeting outcome; correct document identity (publication edition, model generation, status, cutoff); choose one concrete operational example; draft the governing conclusion and material caveat.

**Same day** — build a six-to-ten-page story spine; tag every headline number by evidence state; remove internal comparison history from the opening unless the audience needs it; create one options table and one uncertainty view; move inventories and method detail to assurance.

**Next morning** — run technical, business, language, and design reviews separately; challenge denominator, baseline, causality, implementation status, and role/headcount inference; rehearse the two-minute opening and the five hardest questions.

**Before circulation** — export to PDF and inspect every page at normal size; test links, page numbers, source notes, and confidentiality markings; confirm the decision owner can state what is being asked; freeze the meeting edition and record any post-circulation corrections explicitly.

If time permits only one substantive change, repair page one. If two, add the concrete mechanism. If three, fix evidence labels and caveat placement — these changes usually outperform cosmetic redesign.

## Use AI as an accelerator with explicit controls

AI can inventory sources, cluster risks, draft alternatives, propose structures, rewrite jargon, generate chart variants, and simulate skeptical questions. It can also invent evidence, flatten distinctions, overstate causality, and create polished inconsistency at scale.

| AI task | Human control |
|---|---|
| Extract claims | Compare with source; record provenance |
| Summarize technical work | Check omitted constraints and evidence states |
| Generate narrative options | Choose the argument; reject invented motivation |
| Rewrite for executives | Verify meaning, authority, and modal verbs |
| Propose visuals | Validate data, scales, labels, and accessibility |
| Red-team the brief | Separate plausible challenge from fabricated fact |
| Format the artifact | Inspect every rendered page |

Don't ask AI to "make it more confident" — ask it to make claim strength consistent with evidence. Don't let it convert "modeled" to "expected," "role" to "hire," or "not observed" to "did not happen." The named human owner remains accountable for sources, calculations, judgments, privacy, and release; disclose AI involvement proportionately when it materially assisted analysis or drafting.

## Run four independent reviews

- **Technical truth** — Can the technical owner reproduce every number and defend model boundaries? Are statuses current as of the stated cutoff? Do chart values match tables?
- **Executive usefulness** — Can a reader identify decision, consequence, options, and owner in two minutes? Is motivation present where needed? Does the recommendation address the authority in the room?
- **Language integrity** — Are evidence states, causal verbs, modals, denominators, time horizons, and units correct? Could any sentence be reasonably read more strongly than intended?
- **Design and accessibility** — Does each page have one job? Are hierarchy, contrast, spacing, labels, sources, alt text, and navigation usable? Does the PDF render cleanly?

The same people can run these passes at different times, but keep the passes distinct — a reviewer checking typography will often miss a denominator change, and a technical reviewer immersed in the model may not notice an abstraction gap. End with a claim ledger: headline, evidence state, source, owner, cutoff, material caveat, and approval, so final review runs from a controlled process instead of memory.

## Review through a skeptical reader

Ask a reviewer to adopt five positions and, for each, record the strongest reasonable misreading before arguing the reader "should have known":

1. **The busy executive** — "What is the decision and why now?"
2. **The methodological skeptic** — "Which result depends on an assumption?"
3. **The operational owner** — "Who will do this and what breaks at peak?"
4. **The political reader** — "What does this imply about people, blame, or prior choices?"
5. **The future auditor** — "Can I reconstruct what was known, decided, and approved?"

High-value challenge questions: What concrete event does this category describe? Is this number observed, derived, simulated, or targeted? What is the denominator and baseline? Does the method support causation? What uncertainty could reverse the recommendation? Is the control mapped, deployed, used, or measured? Is this role value, workload, capacity, or headcount? What alternative did we reject and why? What happens if we defer? What will prove the decision worked?

## Design the meeting around the decision

The pre-read and meeting are one system — a good paper can still fail if the session becomes a narrated document review. Open in two minutes: (1) decision and recommendation, (2) motivation and why now, (3) one concrete mechanism, (4) evidence and material uncertainty, (5) questions the group must resolve. Use most of the meeting for challenge and choice — presenters speaking for roughly a third of the allotted time, with the rest for questions and discussion, is a reasonable default ratio. Prepare direct answers to the hardest questions and know which appendix page supports each; if a question reveals a new assumption or decision condition, record it rather than improvising certainty. End by restating the decision in the room: what was approved, rejected, deferred, or conditioned; who owns the next action; by when; what evidence will return. A meeting that ends with "good discussion" but no decision record has not completed the interface.

## Treat release as a governance event

Before distribution, verify: audience and permission boundary; publication edition, analytical generation, status, and date; evidence cutoff and implementation "as of" date; final decision language and approval authority; confidentiality, privacy, and legal-review requirements; source links and attachment completeness; accessible Word and fixed PDF editions; rendered page inspection; distribution list and record location; change procedure after circulation.

If a correction is material, don't silently replace the file — increment the publication edition, describe the change, and notify recipients. If the analytical model changes but the executive conclusion does not, record the model update and explain why the decision remains stable. A "meeting draft" can be polished and still remain a draft: status is not a quality judgment, it communicates authority and permitted reliance.
